Many AI workflows rely on cloud AI. In this model, your device sends a request to a computer in the cloud, which processes the task and sends the result back. Popular AI services and many enterprise solutions work this way. Providers often host AI models in large data centers because the models can require substantial computing power and memory.

For example, when you enter "*Summarize this document*" in a prompt box, these steps occur:

1. Your prompt is sent to a cloud server.
2. The server passes the prompt to an AI model.
3. The model analyzes the text and predicts the most likely response.
4. The response is sent back to your device.

Cloud AI platforms combine computing infrastructure, data storage, AI models, and APIs (application programming interfaces). Together, these resources support tasks that require substantial computing power. Cloud AI also provides access to large-scale computing, model updates, centralized security, and services from many devices.

However, like many solutions that develop over time, there are limits to this model. Many AI services use a metered, or consumption-based, pricing model, where organizations pay for the cloud computing resources needed to process AI requests. As AI use grows and more requests are processed in the cloud, those costs can increase. As a result, organizations are exploring different approaches to distributing AI workloads between cloud and local computing resources.

Microsoft describes this approach as unmetered intelligence: running suitable AI workloads locally on a device with its existing hardware. Instead of sending every task to the cloud, organizations can run routine workloads on devices and reserve cloud AI for scenarios that need larger models or more computing power.

By processing suitable workloads locally, organizations can shift some AI workloads from cloud resources to device hardware while continuing to use cloud AI for scenarios that require additional scale or capabilities.

Local AI processing requires hardware capable of efficiently running AI workloads. Modern Windows devices can include hardware designed to accelerate these workloads.

In addition to traditional CPUs and GPUs, some devices include dedicated AI processing resources, such as Neural Processing Units (NPUs) and GPU neural engines. These specialized resources can perform AI operations efficiently while helping support device performance and power efficiency.

As more AI processing happens locally, organizations also need ways to understand how applications use available hardware resources. IT administrators might need to investigate performance issues, developers might want to verify how an AI workload is being processed, and users might want to understand how AI-enabled applications affect system performance and battery life.

These evolving needs require evolving hardware. In the early days AI-enabled PCs, devices were designed for prosumers and hobbyist creators with an interest in AI tools. Over time those needs have expanded as AI usage becomes more integrated into the way we work.

Surface Laptop Ultra for Business represents the hardware that meets the evolution of the way we work. By combining AI-optimized software features with modern AI hardware, business users can accomplish tasks with AI assistance at rates that were previously unrealistic. In 2025, a four-billion parameter model running on the NPU was perceived to be the ceiling of capability. In 2026, Copilot+ PCs can run a 14-billion parameter model on the NPU with [Aion 1.0 Plan local agentic reasoning](https://blogs.windows.com/windowsdeveloper/2026/06/02/build-2026-furthering-windows-as-the-trusted-platform-for-development/) that delivers both speed and reasoning capability.

Surface Laptop Ultra for Business introduces hardware that takes the capability a step further. Its GPU architecture is designed to support local execution of larger AI models than those typically associated with earlier Copilot+ PC experiences. Depending on the model architecture, quantization method, and workload configuration, supported scenarios can include large language models with up to 120 billion parameters and up to one million tokens of context.
