AI systems are becoming more capable and interconnected.

Earlier AI experiences often focused on helping users generate content, summarize information, answer questions, or find information. These experiences can still require governance, but their impact is often limited to the information they provide to a user.

Agentic AI introduces additional considerations.

An AI agent can use models, tools, data, and applications to complete multi-step tasks. Depending on how it’s designed and deployed, an agent may retrieve information, make decisions, interact with business systems, or take actions on behalf of a user.

The difference becomes clearer when you compare an AI assistant with an AI agent. An AI assistant might draft a response to a customer request. An AI agent might retrieve the customer's information, determine which process applies, create a service ticket, route the ticket to a team, and notify the customer.

The difference is not simply that one system is more advanced than the other. An AI agent has a broader ability to interact with its environment and take actions toward a goal.

## From model behavior to system behavior

An AI model is the component that processes inputs and generates outputs, such as text, classifications, or predictions. A model can be part of a larger AI system that connects it to data, tools, applications, and other services.

As AI systems become more capable, governance needs to account for more than the behavior of an individual model.

A deployed AI system can involve multiple parts of an organization's technology environment:

- AI models
- Platform services
- Applications
- Tools
- Data sources

These systems also interact with users, administrators, and others who may be affected by the system's outputs or actions.

Because an agent can connect to multiple components and interact with users as it completes a task, governance needs to consider the behavior of the entire system, not just the model. The question changes from "Does the model produce an appropriate response?" to questions like:

- What information can the system access?
- What tools can it use?
- What actions can it perform?
- What permissions does it have?
- When does a user need to review or approve an action?
- How can the organization detect unexpected behavior?
- How will governance change as the system or its use changes?

This evolution is a necessary shift toward more dynamic governance as increasingly agentic systems introduce greater interconnection, action, and autonomy.

## Why capability matters

A useful way to evaluate an AI deployment is to consider the relationship between capability and authority.

Capability describes what an AI system can do, such as accessing information, using tools, making decisions, or taking actions. Authority describes what the system is permitted to do within a particular environment.

The difference becomes clearer when you compare two AI deployments with different levels of capability and authority.

- Deployment A uses AI to summarize internal documents for an employee.
- Deployment B uses AI to access internal documents, create records, update business systems, and route requests automatically.

Both deployments use AI. However, Deployment B has a broader operational impact because the AI can interact with business processes and take action. As a system's capability and authority increase, organizations may need additional safeguards and oversight.

This doesn't mean that every agent requires the same controls. Governance should reflect the specific scenario, including the information involved, the actions the system can perform, the authority it has, and the potential impact if the system provides incorrect information, takes unintended actions, accesses information beyond its intended scope, or operates outside expected parameters.

## Key takeaway

As AI becomes more capable of interacting with data, tools, applications, and users, organizations need to consider more than what a model produces when addressing governance. They also need to account for what the AI can do, what it is authorized to do, and how the broader system operates over time.
