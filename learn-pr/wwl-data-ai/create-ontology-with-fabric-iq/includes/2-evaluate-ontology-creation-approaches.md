Creating an ontology means defining business concepts (entity types), their characteristics (properties), and how they connect (relationships).

Fabric IQ offers two paths for creating ontologies. You can generate initial structure from an existing Power BI semantic model, automatically creating entity types and relationships that you then refine. Or you can build directly from OneLake data, manually defining each business concept with full control over naming. Both paths require you to review the definitions and complete the data bindings.

Power BI semantic models organize data for business intelligence. They define tables, columns, measures, and relationships. For example, a semantic model specifies that `patients` links to `rooms` through `CurrentRoomId`, enabling reporting. Ontology generation creates definitions and property bindings from the semantic model. OneLake is Fabric's data lake, where your lakehouse data resides.

## Generate from Power BI semantic model

With this approach, Fabric generates entity types from tables, properties from columns, data bindings, and relationship types. DAX measures appear as metrics whose calculations remain owned by the source semantic model.

:::image type="content" source="../media/power-bi-semantic-model.png" alt-text="Screenshot of Semantic model canvas with five tables and relationship connectors between them." lightbox="../media/power-bi-semantic-model.png":::

This automation saves significant setup time. Within minutes, you have working ontology structure ready to refine into business vocabulary.

Generation creates property data bindings. Entity type keys still need review, and relationship bindings and any required time-series sources need configuration. You need Read and Build permissions on the source semantic model.

You review and refine the generated structure to match how your business communicates. Autogenerated relationship names like `hospitals_has_departments` are technically accurate but might not match your preferred business vocabulary—you can rename them to something like "hospital contains department." The semantic model provides a strong foundation that you adjust to fit your organization's language.

Choose this approach when you have a well-structured semantic model representing your business domain. Ask yourself: Can you explain what each table represents as a business concept? Do relationships reflect how your business works, like "sales occur at stores" or "patients occupy rooms"? Are most tables and relationships relevant to the vocabulary you want? If yes, even with technical names, the model is a good candidate.

## Build directly from OneLake

You can also start with an empty ontology and manually define each entity type before binding it to sources such as lakehouse and eventhouse tables. You define the business vocabulary rather than inheriting the semantic model's structure.

You think through each business concept intentionally—which OneLake data sources populate which entities, how properties should be named, how entities connect through relationships. Every element uses business vocabulary from the beginning, defined exactly as you want it.

This path requires more upfront design work than generating from a Power BI semantic model. It gives you naming control, but you still review the definitions and validate the source mappings. Manual creation doesn't eliminate refinement.

Choose this approach when you don't have a semantic model, when existing models are optimized for reporting rather than business vocabulary, or when you prefer designing ontology structure from scratch. You need clear understanding of your business domain and how entities relate.

The next unit teaches ontology concepts through manual creation—you'll build entity types with properties and keys, then define relationship types. This hands-on approach reveals how ontology structure works. After that, you'll see how semantic model generation automates much of this process. Both approaches lead to the same destination: a complete ontology ready to connect to your data sources.
